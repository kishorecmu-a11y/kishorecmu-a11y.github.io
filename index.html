RESEARCH BACKGROUND

Selecting process configurations under uncertain data

Chemical engineering problems of industrial relevance generally admit many feasible process configurations, each with a distinct capital cost, thermodynamic efficiency and emissions profile, and the data required to discriminate between them is rarely complete. My work addresses this class of problem within process systems engineering, in which a plant is represented as a system of equations and the engineering question becomes which solution of that system justifies capital investment.

Three categories of model recur across the projects presented here. Mathematical programs — mixed-integer linear, mixed-integer quadratically constrained and mixed-integer nonlinear formulations — select among candidate configurations: the daily selection of copper concentrate lots, the activation of a pooling network, and the sizing of an electrolyzer against an intermittent power surplus. Rigorous flowsheet models, developed in Aspen Plus and MATLAB, predict reactor and separation performance together with the associated cost of operation; these have been applied to sustainable aviation fuel synthesis from carbon dioxide, polylactic acid production from lignocellulosic biomass, hydrogen production by reforming and by electrolysis, and the recovery of low-grade waste heat. Numerical and statistical methods address what the first two cannot reach: a finite-volume solver for pollutant dispersion, and supervised learning for molecular screening where quantum chemical evaluation is computationally prohibitive.

A theme common to all of this work is uncertainty in the model inputs. Lot assays carry measurement error; surplus power profiles are forecasts; binding energies inferred from inexpensive descriptors approximate calculations that were never performed. The substantive engineering contribution in each case lies less in increasing model fidelity than in rendering the resulting decision robust to input data that is known to be imperfect.

CU-BLEND · MILP / MIQCP / QCP · WITH AURUBIS AG

Optimal lot selection and calibration for copper blending and smelting

Copper concentrate is delivered by sea from numerous mines and no two lots share the same assay. An optimization model selects the daily combination of lots that maximizes blend tonnage subject to every blend and product specification, including upper bounds on arsenic and other hazardous elements reporting to the flue dust. A second model layer addresses the uncertainty that the optimization cannot observe.

MODEL LAYERS

MODEL 1
MILP · fixed distribution coefficients
MODEL 2
MIQCP · composition-dependent DCp,e
MODEL 3
QCP calibration · ridge and lasso, classifier-gated
STRATEGIES
cumulative · rolling horizon · hybrid
HORIZON
50 forecast days of plant assay data
ERROR
MAPE 0.80% → ≈0.03%

RESULTS

Lot analyses are subject to sampling bias, moisture variation, inaccurate weighing and assays performed infrequently over a limited set of elements. This uncertainty propagates through the blend composition and the distribution coefficients, so that the predicted product analyses deviate systematically from those subsequently reported by the plant.

Further refinement of the model does not address this, since the error originates in the input data. The calibration layer instead estimates the deviation from the historical record of previous predictions and applies the resulting correction to the following day's forecast, with a linear classifier determining whether a correction is warranted.

The mean absolute percentage error of the predicted product analyses decreases from 0.80% to ≈0.03% over a 50-day forecast horizon. The hybrid horizon strategy, in which the training pool grows within each forecast cycle before the base window is advanced, has not previously been reported.

With B. Springub, S. Onyshkevych, C. Galanopoulos (Aurubis AG, Hamburg), J. Wassick and I. E. Grossmann, Department of Chemical Engineering, Carnegie Mellon University.

POOL-MINLP · PYOMO · SCIP

The generalized pooling problem

Five feed streams, four intermediate pools, three products, and the complete set of connections between them. The selection of active streams and pools constitutes a discrete decision, while blending to a quality specification introduces products of flow and concentration. The resulting formulation is a nonconvex mixed-integer nonlinear program, and the superstructure below defines the search space over which it is solved.

FORMULATION

STRUCTURE
5 feeds → 4 pools → 3 products, fully connected
OBJECTIVE
min variable flow cost + fixed activation cost
NONLINEARITY
bilinear flow × concentration at each pool
DISCRETE
binary activation of feeds and intermediates
DEMAND
229 · 173 · 284 kg at 10.5 · 11.2 · 12.5 $/kg
PLATFORM
Pyomo · Intel i7, 10 cores, 2.4 GHz

SOLVER COMPARISON

objective value · axis −4,700 to −4,500 · more negative is better

RESULTS

The same model was submitted to four solvers. SBB, DICOPT and KNITRO each converged to an objective value of −4556.350; SCIP obtained −4640.082, an improvement of approximately 1.8%, at roughly three times the execution time.

The agreement of three solvers does not establish global optimality for a nonconvex problem; it indicates convergence to a common local solution. SCIP's spatial branch-and-bound resolves this, at the cost of computation time.

The optimum for Example 1 activates feeds 1, 2 and 5 into pools 1 and 3 only, pools 2 and 4 remaining inactive. Example 2 introduces a third quality component and tighter flow bounds; the solution structure changes to four feeds and three pools, with a best objective of −3849.447, again obtained by SCIP.

The solutions reproduce, and in places marginally improve upon, the benchmark values reported by Lee and Grossmann (2003).

With Amogh Amonkar. 06-720 Advanced Process Systems Engineering, Carnegie Mellon University.

H2-OPT · LINEAR PROGRAMMING · CPLEX

Hydrogen production from surplus renewable electricity

Curtailed wind and solar generation represents electricity for which no payment is received. This project determines the appropriate capacity of a PEM electrolyzer intended to absorb it, and demonstrates that the answer depends on the objective adopted. Three formulations share a common model of an electrolyzer and compressor, and yield materially different designs.

FORMULATIONS

PROBLEM 1
max NPV at fixed $H₂
PROBLEM 2
max Σ ṁH₂,t subject to NPV ≥ 0
PROBLEM 3
min $H₂ subject to Σ ṁ·Δt ≥ H2,target
VARIABLES
MH₂ capacity · ṁH₂,t hourly rate
KEY CONSTRAINT
(fpwr,el + fpwr,comp)·ṁH₂,t ≤ REt
SOLVER
linearized to LP · CPLEX

RESULTS

Problem 3 is not linear as originally posed, since price and production rate are both decision variables and appear as a product in the revenue term. Imposing the production target as an equality constraint reduces that product to a constant and renders the formulation linear.

Problem 1 operates the electrolyzer at a near-constant rate, absorbing a fixed fraction of the available surplus. Problem 2 follows availability hour by hour, saturates the surplus constraint, and falls to zero during hours 21 and 22, when the surplus is exhausted.

Over the price range $5–10/kg, the optimal capacity increases from 0 to 3.5 MW under the profit objective and from 0.5 to 2.2 MW under the price-minimizing objective. Increasing the annual production target from 35,000 to 140,000 kg reduces the breakeven price from $10.0 to $6.5/kg as fixed costs are distributed over a larger output.

FT-SAF · ASPEN PLUS · PR-BM

Sustainable aviation fuel from carbon dioxide by Fischer–Tropsch synthesis

A complete carbon-dioxide-to-jet-fuel flowsheet developed in Aspen Plus. A reverse water–gas shift reactor converts the carbon dioxide feed to synthesis gas at the hydrogen-to-carbon-monoxide ratio required downstream; a cobalt-catalyzed Fischer–Tropsch reactor produces a broad C1–C44 hydrocarbon distribution; and the resulting wax is hydrocracked and hydrogenated into the C8–C16 aviation range. Vapor–liquid equilibrium is described throughout by the Peng–Robinson equation of state with the Boston–Mathias modification.

OPERATING CONDITIONS

R-WGS
950 °C · 25 bar · Ni catalyst
FT-REACT
220 °C · 25 bar · Co catalyst
R-CRACK
350 °C · 35 bar · Pt/zeolite
R-HYDRO
300 °C · 40 bar · Al₂O₃
ASF MODEL
α = 0.85 · olefin/paraffin c = 0.30
CONVERSION
85% CO per pass · 1% CO₂ selectivity
FEED
10,000 kg/h CO₂ · 1,402 kg/h H₂

RESULTS

The Fischer–Tropsch reactor is implemented as an RStoic block. The Anderson–Schulz–Flory weight fractions were therefore normalized to C42 for paraffins and C24 for olefins, then converted to molar extents on a 1000 g basis before being specified as stoichiometric conversions.

The flowsheet produces 194 kg/h of kerosene, 330 kg/h of naphtha and 203 kg/h of diesel, corresponding to 1,700, 2,890 and 1,781 t/yr respectively.

The diesel yield agrees with the reference study; the kerosene and naphtha yields do not, the reference reporting 1,812 and 686 kg/h from an equivalent feed. The product distribution is reproduced correctly while the absolute rates are low. The discrepancy is attributed to the hydrocracking and hydrogenation sections, whose operating conditions were optimized in the reference study but not in the present work.

PLA-TEA · GUTHRIE COSTING · GREENSCOPE

Polylactic acid from eucalyptus: techno-economic and sustainability assessment

Two linked flowsheets. The first converts Eucalyptus grandis to purified lactic acid through pretreatment, enzymatic hydrolysis and Lactobacillus fermentation; the second polymerizes the lactic acid to high-molecular-weight polylactic acid by way of oligomerization and lactide formation. Both were sized and costed in the Aspen Process Economic Analyzer and subsequently evaluated on a gate-to-gate basis against GREENSCOPE sustainability indicators.

CONDITIONS AND COSTING

HYDROLYSIS
48 °C · atmospheric · cellulase
FERM
45 °C · anaerobic · 90% yield
OLIGOMR
170 °C · 400 mmHg
YIELDDP
200 °C · 10 mmHg · tin octanoate

170 °C · 760 mmHg · ring-opening

BASIS

GREENSCOPE SUSTAINABILITY SCORE

percent of best-case score · 0–100%

RESULTS

At a production rate of 76,610 t/yr sold at $2,500/t, the plant returns $191.5 M/yr against a total capital investment of $37.26 M. The breakeven selling price solves to $2,460.72/t, leaving a margin of approximately $40 per tonne.

The lowest-scoring indicator is chronic human health toxicity at 50.54%, attributable to the use of tin octanoate as the depolymerization catalyst. Substitution of this catalyst would improve the sustainability profile more substantially than any degree of additional heat integration.

The second-lowest indicator, hazardous material mass at 64.46%, corresponds to 15,384 kg/h of hazardous input, or 1.74 kg per kilogram of product. Both weak indicators originate in the polymerization section rather than the fermentation section.

H2-EXERGY · MATLAB · CANTERA

Steam methane reforming and PEM electrolysis compared on an exergetic basis

An energy balance alone does not discriminate between these two hydrogen production routes; an exergy analysis accounts for the available work and charges each irreversibility. Two thermodynamic models — a steam methane reformer with shift conversion and membrane separation, and a proton exchange membrane electrolyzer including its complete overpotential stack — were developed in MATLAB against Cantera property data and evaluated on a common basis.

MODEL PARAMETERS

REFORMER
900–1200 K · 5–25 bar · Ni catalyst
SHIFT
473–673 K, then 400–450 K
MEMBRANE
573–873 K · Δp 1–40 atm · metallic
PEM CELL
313 K · L = 100 µm · J = 5,000 A/m²
OVERPOTENTIAL
Nernst + ohmic + activation (anode and cathode)
MEMBRANE λ
λa = 14 → λc = 10, linear in x
HX EFFICIENCY
ε = 0.90 · T₀ = 298.15 K

EXERGY EFFICIENCY

ηex, percent · common basis and dead state

RESULTS

The reformer converts 1,000 g/s of methane to 262 g/s of hydrogen at an exergy efficiency of 57%. The losses are structural rather than the consequence of poor design: high-temperature reforming is inherently irreversible and methane conversion is incomplete.

The PEM electrolyzer attains 60%, and does so from renewable electricity rather than natural gas. Its efficiency falls as current density increases and is sensitive to membrane thickness: at 15,000 A/m² the computed cell voltage is approximately 2.3 V for a 50 µm membrane against 3.2 V for 200 µm, the difference arising from the ohmic overpotential.

Reforming therefore retains an advantage in throughput and technological maturity, while electrolysis is superior in both exergy efficiency and carbon intensity. The principal design variable for the electrolyzer is the achievable membrane thickness rather than the choice of catalyst.

ORC-WHR · COOLPROP · IN PROGRESS

Organic Rankine cycles for industrial waste heat recovery

Industrial waste heat is available over a wide temperature range, from approximately 200 °C at sintering machines to 1,800 °C at electric arc furnaces, and the greater part of it is rejected. An organic Rankine cycle can recover heat below 400 °C, where a conventional steam cycle is not viable. This project develops a component-level thermodynamic model of the cycle and compares working fluids and configurations against the characteristics of the heat source.

MODEL

COMPONENTS
pump · boiler · turbine · condenser
PROPERTIES
CoolProp, per working fluid
HEAT CARRIER
air, per unit mass flow of cooled carrier
SOURCE BANDS
low < 400 °C · medium 400–700 °C · high > 700 °C
OUTPUT METRIC
electric power per unit carrier mass flow
PARAMETRIC STUDY
fluids × configurations × source temperature

STATUS

Thermodynamic equations have been formulated for each component and coupled through CoolProp property calls, permitting a configuration to be evaluated across heat source temperatures and working fluids without manual recalculation of the state points.

The problem is one of selection rather than of single-point design: for a given source temperature and required output, which working fluid and which cycle configuration. The case for the organic Rankine cycle rests on its tolerance of a wide source temperature range, its scalability to distributed generation, and the commercial availability of the required equipment.

Work in progress. The module characterization study — heat source temperature, power output and thermal efficiency — remains ongoing, and no efficiency figure is reported here.

With Ekaterina Dragoshanskaia.

CFD-PLUME · FLP-ML

CFD-PLUME · FRACTIONAL STEP · STAGGERED GRID

Dispersion of transit bus exhaust and the implied safe following distance

On a per-passenger basis a transit bus is a low-emission mode of transport; on a per-vehicle basis it is not, and a cyclist travelling behind one occupies its wake. A two-dimensional incompressible solver was developed from first principles: the Navier–Stokes equations on a staggered grid with ghost nodes, advanced by the fractional step method, with a scalar transport equation carrying the exhaust plume through the computed velocity field.

NUMERICAL SETUP

DOMAIN
30 × 6 m · dx = dy = 0.25 m
TIME STEP
1 × 10⁻⁴ s · CFL-limited upwinding
KIN. VISCOSITY
1.46 × 10⁻⁴ m²/s
DIFFUSIVITY
1.39 × 10⁻⁴ m²/s
INLET
u = 5 m/s, v = 0 (Dirichlet)
EXHAUST
10 m/s · c = 1 over the uppermost 0.5 m
VEHICLE
no-slip, ∂p/∂n = 0, zero plume except at the tailpipe

PLUME AT BREATHING-ZONE HEIGHT

normalized concentration 1.5 m above the underside of the vehicle, against distance behind it

RESULTS

The velocity field exhibits the expected features: reverse flow at the leading upper corner where the flow separates, an accelerated stream beneath the vehicle in the low-clearance configuration, and a shedding wake downstream. The plume is advected and diffused into that wake rather than rising clear of it.

At a height of 1.5 m above the underside of the vehicle, taken as representative of a cyclist's breathing zone, the concentration remains elevated over the first several metres, decreases sharply between 5 and 6 m, and is negligible beyond 8 m. A minimum following distance of 10 m at low speed is recommended.

Limitations. The pressure solution did not converge to an acceptable tolerance on a grid of this resolution, and that failure is the origin of the numerical instability which ultimately terminates the simulation: scenario 1 remains stable to approximately 1 s and scenario 2 to 1.2 s. Successive over-relaxation, non-uniform or adaptive grid refinement, consistent upwinding and explicit turbulence modeling are identified as the necessary remedies; until these are implemented the model is restricted to travel speeds of approximately 5 m/s.

With Aditya Shailendra Khatu and Miguel Lanus Valdivia, Carnegie Mellon University.

FLP-ML · RDKIT · xTB · SCIKIT-LEARN

Machine learning for carbon dioxide activation by frustrated Lewis pairs

A frustrated Lewis pair comprises an acid and a base too sterically hindered to form an adduct, so that both sites remain reactive and may together bind and activate carbon dioxide without a metal centre. The design space is large and quantum chemical evaluation of every candidate is computationally prohibitive. The question addressed is whether inexpensive electronic and steric descriptors predict binding strength with sufficient accuracy to rank candidates.

DATASET AND FEATURES

STRUCTURES
133 FLPs · 127 usable after cleaning
SPLIT
101 train / 26 test
ELECTRONIC
HOMO · LUMO · gap, from xTB
STERIC
Sterimol L · B1 · B5, from morFeus
TARGET
CO₂ binding energy, −163 to +238 kJ/mol
MODELS
linear · ridge · lasso · elastic net · SVR · RF · GBR

RANDOM FOREST FEATURE IMPORTANCE

mean decrease in impurity · fraction of total

RESULTS

All linear models fail on the held-out set, returning negative coefficients of determination and therefore performing worse than the sample mean. The binding energy is not a linear function of the six descriptors.

The tree ensembles perform considerably better. Random forest achieves RMSE 33.0 kJ/mol and R² 0.41; gradient boosting achieves 40.3 and 0.12, against a target range of approximately 400 kJ/mol.

The feature importances are chemically consistent: the LUMO and HOMO energies rank first and second, as expected where the Lewis acid accepts electron density from the carbon dioxide carbon and the base donates into an oxygen, while Sterimol B5, the maximum steric extent, ranks third. Frustration is a geometric property, and the model resolves it.

An R² of 0.41 does not constitute a predictive model. It is adequate as a screening filter, ranking a large candidate set so that expensive calculations may be directed to the highest-ranked entries, which was the stated objective.

With Aditya Khatu and Soham Joshi.
